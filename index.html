Read this first The inlet ratings on this page are a rough guide built from forecasts, not an observation of the sandbar and not a substitute for looking at it. Conditions at Ponce change with the sandbar, with a squall, and with a swell the forecast never saw. NEVER RUN THIS INLET ON THIS APP ALONE. How to read this board
Day buttons
Pick any day up to a week out. Everything below updates to that day. The dot on each button is the best inlet rating that day, so you can see at a glance which day is worth going.
Inlet run windows
When the tide runs out through the inlet it pushes east, straight into any sea coming from the east — and a wave meeting an opposing current gets shorter, steeper and taller. That is what stands the sandbar up. This card walks the day hour by hour, works out how hard the current is running and which way the sea is going, and marks the stretches where the two are not fighting each other. A flooding tide with the same sea is usually flat, which is why the good windows so often sit on the incoming water.
Weather
Day's high, night's low, rain chance and sky for Daytona Beach, Port Orange, Ponce Inlet and New Smyrna Beach, from the National Weather Service, for whichever day is selected. Any watch or warning in force for coastal Volusia or the waters out to 20 nm — a small craft advisory, a thunderstorm warning — shows in red at the top of the card. No red block does not mean all clear: if the alert feed cannot be reached, the card simply has nothing to show.
Radar & satellite
Live radar over the satellite picture, framed on this coast — St. Augustine to Melbourne, inland past Ocala, about 50 nm offshore, with Daytona Beach, Port Orange, New Smyrna Beach and Ponce Inlet marked. It loads only when you open it, because it costs about 0.3 MB a look; tap Refresh for the current frame. Frames are published every 5 minutes, so the newest one can be a few minutes old.
Feeding times (tan card)
Majors are ~2-hour windows when the moon is directly overhead or underfoot; minors are ~1-hour windows around moonrise and moonset. Solunar theory says fish feed most actively in these windows — strongest near a new or full moon. The top right of the card draws tonight's moon at its actual phase: filled is the lit side, and it lights on the right as the moon waxes, on the left as it wanes.
Tide curve
Bold amber = outgoing (ebb) water. Thin gray = incoming (flood). The vertical dashed red line is a clock hand: it marks the current time on today's curve, so where it crosses the curve is where the tide stands right now. It only appears on Today's view.
Depth now (blue strip)
The depth of water at that spot at this minute — the surveyed bottom plus the tide standing on it — and what it falls to at the day's lowest low. This is the water under your boat, which is not the number in the High/Low table below it. Read the caution under the board before you trust it: these are survey depths, not chart soundings.
▼ Ebbing / ▲ Flooding
What the water is doing at that spot this minute, and when it turns next. An amber-bordered card means that spot is ebbing right now.
High/Low table
That day's turn times and heights at that spot. A height is feet of tide above MLLW — how far the water has risen off its own zero, not how much water is under you. A 0.5 ft low does not mean six inches to run in: for that read the blue depth strip. "No turns this day" means the highs/lows fall just before midnight or just after — the neighboring days have them.
Lag line
Upriver spots run behind the inlet. Tap the lag line on any card to adjust its high/low delay after you've compared a prediction to real water. Saved automatically.
Add a spot
The button under the cards opens the waypoint list from the WTF chart — every named creek, bay, bridge and island from Tomoka Basin down to Mosquito Lagoon. Pick one and it gets a card with an estimated lag worked out from how far it sits from the inlet. Calibrate it the same way as the rest.
Remove a card
Any card can come off the board: tap its lag line and use Remove card. The built-in ones are only hidden — their lag, sounding and calibration are kept, and they come back from Removed cards at the top of the Add-a-spot list. A spot you added yourself is deleted outright, but the waypoint list can always make it again.

Times. Upriver times are the inlet prediction shifted by a lag for each spot. The Dunlawton Bridge lag is informed by measured tide-gauge phase data from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Ponce de Leon Inlet site investigation (Technical Report CHL-99-1, 1995–97 field study). Other lags marked est. are educated estimates — tap any lag to adjust it once you've compared a predicted high or low against what you see on the water. Your adjustments are saved on this device. Predictions are astronomical; strong wind or heavy rain runoff will move the real water — and not by a little. One windy day in the DJM calibration record put low water 77 minutes late against an otherwise steady run of days at that spot. Heights are in feet above MLLW (Mean Lower Low Water) — the long-term average of each day's lower low tide, the zero mark used on U.S. charts. A negative height means water below even that average low.

Depths. The "Depth now" figures come from NOAA BlueTopo and NCEI CUDEM survey grids, contoured to the foot, plus the tide standing on them. Since build 16 those contours are measured to MLLW — the same zero as the tide heights above, and the same one your chartplotter uses. They are not chart soundings, the surveys are years old in places, and this coast shoals and moves. Use them to find an edge or a hole worth fishing. Read your depth sounder for the water under your boat. The shallow water in these grids is mapped by laser from an aircraft, which gets no return through tannic water — so a deep hole can drop out of the survey and be filled in from the bar around it, and the survey will call it shallow with no hint that it is guessing. That is why a sounding you enter yourself always outranks the survey on these cards. So trust your eyes where you can see clean sand, and trust your sounder everywhere else.

DEPTHS ON THIS BOARD ARE FOR PLANNING PURPOSES, NOT FOR NAVIGATION.

Wind and sea. Wind and wave forecasts are the National Weather Service gridded forecast for the inlet (Melbourne office); the wind for the hour around now is the live reading from the New Smyrna Beach airport anemometer 2.5 nm away, or Daytona at 9.6 nm when that one is not reporting. Present sea state and swell direction are the live reading from NDBC buoy 41070, 13.8 nm off the inlet. NDBC publishes no header that lets a web page read it directly, so that one reading comes in through a small relay this board runs itself; if the card says it could not be had, the data is still NOAA's and it is the relay, the buoy or your signal that is missing, never a different source substituted for it. The NWS grid does not publish a wave direction forecast for this point, so beyond the buoy's current reading the board assumes the sea follows the forecast wind — good for a local summer chop, poor for a distant ground swell. Every window says which of the two it was built on.

Every number on this page is NOAA's. Tides and datums from NOS/CO-OPS; depth from NCEI and the Office of Coast Survey (BlueTopo, CUDEM); wind, waves and the airport observations from the National Weather Service; sea state from the National Data Buoy Center. The one exception is the radar and satellite picture, which is NOAA data — GOES and NEXRAD — but drawn and served by the Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University meteorology department in Daytona Beach, whose sector happens to be centred on this coast. Nothing here is bought, scraped from a commercial forecast, or inferred from a source that cannot be named — where a figure is Jan's own measurement rather than NOAA's, such as a calibrated lag or a sounded depth, the card says so on its face. Adjust lag

Minutes after the inlet tide. Highs and lows travel upriver at different speeds, so each gets its own lag. Watch one real high or low at this spot, compare to the inlet time, and set the difference here.

Depth you have actually seen here at dead low, if you know it. A figure you have sounded beats the survey every time — the survey grids miss docks, holes and dredged cuts, and around here they miss whole channels. Leave blank to use the survey.

Peak current at the inlet

NOAA publishes no current prediction for Ponce, so the board works the current out from how fast the tide is rising or falling and scales it to this figure at the strongest part of the run. If the inlet runs harder or softer than the ratings suggest, set what you actually see here. Typical spring ebb at Ponce is 3–4 knots.
